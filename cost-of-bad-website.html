The Real Cost of a Bad Website (And How to Avoid It)

2026-03-036 min readBranket Solutions

A bad website rarely fails loudly. Nobody calls to complain that your site took nine seconds to load — they just leave, and you never find out why the enquiry never came in.

Slow is the same as broken

Most visitors give a page a few seconds before deciding whether to stay. A website that takes too long to load isn't "a bit slow" from a business perspective — it's functionally the same as not having a website at all for everyone who leaves before it finishes loading.

Confusing navigation quietly loses customers

If a visitor can't find pricing, services, or a way to contact you within a few clicks, most won't dig further — they'll assume the information doesn't exist, or go find a competitor whose site made it obvious.

Common quiet failures

  • No clear way to contact the business from the homepage
  • Text that's too small or low-contrast to read comfortably on mobile
  • Outdated information — old prices, discontinued services, old team members
  • A design so generic it doesn't build any distinct impression at all
  • Forms that don't actually work, silently dropping enquiries

Old design reads as an old business

Fairly or not, an outdated-looking website makes people question whether the business itself is still active, still capable of quality work, and still worth the risk of a first order.

What "good enough" actually looks like

A website doesn't need to win design awards. It needs to load quickly, clearly explain what you do, make it obvious how to get in touch, and not actively work against the goodwill you've already built with your customers elsewhere.

The fix is rarely a total rebuild. Usually it's a handful of specific, fixable problems — a slow image, a missing phone number, a confusing menu — that are cheap to fix and expensive to leave alone.
